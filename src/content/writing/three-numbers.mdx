<div class="article-intro wrap">

"We need a dashboard."

"Lovely! If it could only show three numbers, which would they be?"

There's a pause. Eventually, they say, "We don't know. We're not really data people."

I've had this conversation, or something very much like it, with more clients than you'd think. It's never a problem. It's the most useful moment in the whole engagement, because the answer matters more than anything we're about to build.

</div>

<Chapter id="i-can-see-clearly-now" kicker="I can see clearly now">

This spring I presented the first draft of a dashboard to a museum and gallery I work with. They were lovely about it. They liked how it looked and how it worked, but what I remember most is the relief. Finally, they could look at one page, see everything they needed, and move on.

"Move on" is exactly what I want them to do. An organisation that knows what normal looks like can see that things are normal at a glance and get on with its day. The best dashboards are the ones you can stop looking at. And once an organisation can see, it wants to see more. By late summer they were asking about audience and marketing data too.

That relief didn't come from building a dashboard. They weren't impressed by my inventive use of context transition or ability to bend Power BI's formatting to my will. It came from the work we did before building even began. They set up a workshop to agree the metrics. A report defined each one and flagged where the data was shaky. We trawled through the ticketing, the tills and the online shop to find out where the numbers really lived. By the time I built anything, I knew them well enough to know what they wanted to see. My job was to show it in a way they'd understand intuitively.

Over the summer I spent an hour, as a volunteer, with a mental health charity. They had a CRM where anyone could build their own reports and dashboards, and nobody was sure they could trust it. To check a number, you'd look it up and then message someone on Slack. Their head of programmes admitted she couldn't "see beyond what we already do."

We didn't build anything. Over the hour we talked about what they wanted to learn, which of their data they'd be most nervous to build on, and how you'd tell whether data had influenced a decision at all. I sketched what a map of their evidence might look like. It would show which sources were strong, which were weak and where to start. Their impact lead said, "I can just already see how much relief I'm feeling." At the end, she said the questions made her "think more deeply about things".

They felt the same relief, and we hadn't built a dashboard. The relief comes from the doing. The dashboard usually gets the credit because it's standing nearest when the relief arrives.

I like the building. I enjoy writing DAX, and yes, I know how that sounds. But the building was never why. I don't do BI to make as many dashboards as possible. I do it because helping an organisation see is a reward in itself, especially when it's the kind of organisation that calls itself "not data people".

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="so-whats-new" kicker="So what's new?">

At FabCon in Barcelona at the end of September, Microsoft showed Power BI Desktop for Apps building apps on request. Describe what you want and it builds one on top of your semantic model, ready to publish. Ten days later, Anthropic launched Claude Dashboards (now Claude Data). Connect your warehouse or your CRM, ask a question, and get a live dashboard that keeps itself up to date.

It has always been possible to get a dashboard just by asking. That's what contractors and consultants, people like me, were for! You could insist on, you know, a dashboard, with numbers on charts, and somebody would build it. What's changed is that you can have it right now.

The doing came bundled with the build. Writing a measure forced someone to say what "active customers" meant. Laying out a page forced a choice about what mattered most, and showing it to a colleague forced an explanation. No value was ever in the DAX. Don't mistake the doing for the building.

A quick dashboard can help with all of that. Put something in front of people and they'll tell you what's missing, what doesn't look right, or what they'd expected to see instead. That's doing the work too. The trouble starts when having the dashboard means you think you've finished.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="lies-damned-lies-and-dashboards" kicker="Lies, damned lies, and dashboards">

A dashboard used to take hours if not days. Colleagues saw it on the way through. Somebody slept on it. That didn't make it a good report, but someone had time to say "oh, no, wait, this is a bad idea", or to notice that you were building from a conclusion backwards.

Now the dashboard exists a minute after the question does. It can be perfectly right. But its existence alone doesn't tell you that someone has thought about it.

And it reads beautifully. Things that are easier to read feel truer. A live, tidy dashboard looks like finished work. It's easy to look at one and think "job done". Those of us who build reports always knew how many choices went into one. Everyone else sees the result. Now there may be nobody who has examined those choices, including the person who asked for it.

Put those together and any opinion that can be marginally supported by data can now have a convincing dashboard on demand. The highest-paid person in the room finally has an analyst of their own. Now everyone else has one too.

This is still not new. Any team who want the same answer could happily build a dashboard of motivated reasoning together. But a colleague at least had to be persuaded. An agent can be easy to persuade. It was trained on human preferences, and humans like being agreed with. I've never heard "sorry bud, that's an awful idea" from an agent. I've definitely heard it from a colleague.

Checking a dashboard also takes longer than generating one. The person who questions it has to spend the time finding out what the numbers mean and how they were calculated.

At the extreme, AI can give everyone a dashboard of their own, tuned to their own questions. Here data democracy tips into demagoguery. Everyone has evidence, nobody has agreement, and the argument goes to whoever is most fluent or most senior.

What gets lost is the shared sense of normal that let the museum team glance at a page and get on with their day. You build it by arguing about definitions and looking at the same numbers, together, for long enough to know when one is off. Somebody says "that's odd", and everyone knows what they're looking at.

A fresh dashboard for each question can give you answers without ever giving you a normal. A dashboard for each person, with nobody comparing notes, gives everyone their own normal, which comes to the same thing as nobody having one.

Automate a process and the people can be left with the hardest parts, and less practice at them than ever. Automate the build and what's left is the doing, without the questions and conversations the build used to force.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="kicking-and-screaming" kicker="Kicking and screaming">

So the doing has to happen on purpose now. That means saying why you're doing analytics at all and talking about what the organisation takes for granted. Some things go unsaid because nobody's ever had to say them out loud, and some because saying them starts a fight.

None of this needs a BI team. It works for the three people who make up the entire finance and reporting function of plenty of good organisations.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="say-why-before-anything-gets-built" kicker="Say why before anything gets built">

There are two good answers: a decision this will feed, or a normal it will teach. The three-numbers question is the quickest way to find out which, and whether anyone knows. If nobody can answer, you've found the work.

For the charity, we scoped the follow-up around one decision they have to make in the next few months. The plan was to find the three to five questions it turns on, and map nothing that doesn't bear on them. Build the dashboard anyway if you like, since it's only a sentence, but don't mistake having it for having done it.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="find-out-what-has-been-decided-for-you" kicker="Find out what has been decided for you">

Every dashboard includes choices about what counts, which dates, and what got left out. A prompt-to-dashboard tool can make those choices without asking and produce something that looks sensible. Know what you decided and what you didn't.

Put the first version in front of the people who know the work. Find the number two people interpret differently. Ask what's missing. That list is the agenda for the conversation the dashboard let you skip.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="run-a-pre-mortem" kicker="Run a pre-mortem">

Before you start, imagine the thing has already failed, and have everyone write down why. Something like "nobody has opened this since March". For a decision, try "we acted on this and it went badly".

Imagining that the failure has already happened helps people name reasons that "what might go wrong?" can miss. Writing them down alone first lets people raise doubts without looking disloyal. What comes out is a list of things people hadn't said: the definition two people hold differently, the data everyone knows is patchy, the decision nobody has the authority to make.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="make-the-counter-dashboard-ordinary" kicker="Make the counter-dashboard ordinary">

"What evidence would show we're wrong?" is just a prompt now. The hard part is wanting to use it. So make it somebody's job.

Before acting on a dashboard, have the person proposing the decision finish two sentences: "We think this because..." and "We'd change our minds if...". Then give a colleague the job of checking one plausible reason the conclusion might not hold. If rising demand is the case for hiring, does the rise survive taking out that one exceptional week?

Agree the check before looking at its result. Use the AI to help run it against the source data. Keep the definitions consistent and make any changed filters explicit. Ask to see the calculation and the records behind it. A plausible explanation is something to investigate. It isn't something you've found.

The colleague brings back what the check showed, what remains unknown, and whether the proposed action still follows. Put that beside the original dashboard before deciding. Rotate the job next time.

It's a culture thing. It's always a culture thing. Organisations create incentives, and incentives create a culture. If the person who questions the dashboard gets treated as an obstacle, don't expect them to keep doing it. Making the awkward question somebody's job only works if asking it is valued.

And "the AI agrees with me" still doesn't count as a second opinion.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="keep-fewer-dashboards-for-longer-and-look-at-them-together" kicker="Keep fewer dashboards, for longer, and look at them together">

A useful sense of normal needs repetition with feedback. A fresh dashboard for every question never gives anyone the repetition.

Explore as much as you like. Keep coming back to a few shared measures, and check what happened against what you expected. That's how you learn when to glance and move on, and when to stop and ask what's happened.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="plus-ca-change" kicker="Plus ça change">

The point of analytics was never a Power BI report. It's deciding which data matters, agreeing what it means, learning what normal looks like and choosing what you'll do about it. Every generation of tools has made the outputs cheaper. The work that makes them worth having still has to happen.

Data democracy is more people taking part in the doing, including the ones who swear they're not data people. Data demagoguery is more people skipping it, each with a dashboard to wave. The tools don't decide which you get. All they've done is remove the excuse that the building was the hard part.

The organisation at the top of this essay can have its dashboard by teatime. Its three numbers will take longer, and they're the part worth having. They might even find out they were data people all along.
